# Project rules — B. Games (TypeScript + Three.js)

Applies to: every real-time 3D project in this repo. Target is **maximum
realism** — graphics, materials, physics and feel — at a locked frame rate.

Read `AGENT.md` first. This file adds to it and wins where they disagree.

Target machine: a desktop with a high-end GPU (RTX 5090 class, 32 GB VRAM) on
Windows, in a Chromium browser with WebGPU (WebGL2 as fallback). Design for
that, then provide a Low preset that still holds 60 fps on a laptop.

---

## 0. Reference sheets — look at these before you tune anything

All six live in `references/`. They are generated by
`references/_generate_references.py`, so they can be regenerated, not guessed.

| `scale-reference.png` | true size of soldier, tank, jet and helicopter on one scale, plus city grey-box dimensions and eye heights |

**Rule:** if you pick a colour, a roughness value, a light intensity, a vehicle
size or a HUD position that disagrees with these sheets, say why in a comment.

Drop any extra art references you gather into `references/` as `.png` or `.jpg`

## 1. Realism means physical units

Everything is SI. Every number in the game world means something real.

- Distance in **metres**, mass in **kilograms**, time in **seconds**,
  angles in **radians** internally (degrees only at the UI boundary).
- One Three.js world unit = **one metre**. Never rescale a model to "look right".
  If it looks wrong, the reference sheet says which one of you is wrong.
- Gravity is `9.81 m/s²`. Air density at sea level is `1.225 kg/m³`.
- Speed variables carry units in the name: `speedMs`, `altitudeM`, `rateRadS`.
- Light variables carry units too: `sunIlluminanceLux`, `flashIntensityCd`,
  `bulbFluxLm`. Directional lights in lux, point and spot lights in candela.
- Never mix a unit and a "game feel multiplier" in the same value. Keep the
  physical number, then apply a named, commented tuning factor separately.

## 2. Rendering

### 2.1 Renderer

- `WebGPURenderer` (from `three/webgpu`) with TSL is the main path. It falls back
  to its WebGL2 backend by itself, so there is one code path, not two.
  If a needed effect only exists for `WebGLRenderer`, say so and ask.
- Output colour space `SRGBColorSpace`. Tone mapping `ACESFilmicToneMapping` is
  the default. Keep an A/B switch to `AgXToneMapping`: ACES shifts bright
  saturated colours (fire turns yellow, afterburner turns white, skies go
  cyan). Pick per game after a side-by-side screenshot, and write the choice down.
- With a post chain, `renderer.toneMapping` is **not** applied to render targets.
  Tone mapping and sRGB conversion happen once, in the output stage
  (`OutputPass` on WebGL, the output node on WebGPU). Never twice, never zero.
- Lights are physical by default in current Three.js. Do not set
  `useLegacyLights` — it was removed in r165 and is a type error in strict TS.
- HDR render targets are half float. Render scale is a setting: 1.0 default,
  1.25–1.5 on High (the browser has no DLSS, so supersampling is the best AA
  we have).

### 2.2 Exposure — do this before any other lighting work

- Lights are authored in physical units from `lighting-reference.png`
  (sun 100 000 lx at noon, not "intensity 3").
- **Pre-exposure:** multiply every light intensity (and the sky and emissive
  values) by `exposure = 1 / (1.2 · 2^EV100)` *before* rendering
  (Lagarde & de Rousiers, "Moving Frostbite to PBR", 2014). Without it, a sun
  highlight on polished metal or the sun disc overflows FP16 (max 65504) and
  leaves black or white pixels that TAA smears across the frame.
- **Auto exposure (eye adaptation)** from a luminance histogram, with separate
  speeds for bright-to-dark (slow, ~2 s) and dark-to-bright (fast, ~0.5 s).
  Clamp EV100 per map so night never becomes day. Walking from a street into
  a building interior must work without any manual trick.
- Do not fix a dark scene by raising ambient. Fix exposure, then light.

### 2.3 Lights, sky, shadows

- Sun is a single `DirectionalLight` with cascaded shadow maps (`CSM` addon on
  WebGL, `CSMShadowNode` on WebGPU). 4 cascades for open terrain, 2 for
  interiors. Tune the split against the real play distance, not the default.
- Soft shadow penumbra sized from the sun's angular diameter (0.53°), so
  shadows sharpen at contact and soften with distance. Add screen-space contact
  shadows for small detail (bolts, rivets, grass at the feet).
- Sky is a physical atmosphere, never a flat colour or a single static HDRI in
  outdoor maps. The three.js `Sky` (Preetham) model is only valid from the
  ground; any game that flies uses a LUT-based model (Hillaire, EGSR 2020) that
  works from ground to high altitude.
- **Aerial perspective** on every object, not only on the sky: distant things
  pick up in-scattered sky colour and lose contrast. This is the main cue for
  distance and scale beyond ~300 m. Height fog is part of the same model and
  follows the lighting preset.
- Volumetric light (god rays through trees, dust, smoke) on High, shadowed by
  the sun's cascade.

### 2.4 Global illumination and reflections

- A single environment map lights interiors as if they were outdoors. That is
  the biggest "game look" error in city maps. Interiors get **baked lightmaps
  or baked irradiance probes** (exported with the glTF), plus a small set of
  **box-projected reflection probes** per room. Outdoors, the atmosphere
  provides the ambient.
- Screen-space reflections on High for wet asphalt, puddles, glass and water.
  Without SSR, a wet road reflects only the sky. SSR falls back to the nearest
  reflection probe, never to black.
- Specular occlusion from AO so reflections do not glow inside cavities.

### 2.5 Post chain, in this order

1. Opaque pass with a **velocity buffer** (all skinned, instanced and animated
   meshes write motion vectors — otherwise TAA ghosts on foliage and rotors).
2. AO (GTAO) — applied to **indirect light only**, never to direct sun.
   Multiplying the final colour gives dirty halos in full sunlight.
3. SSR.
4. Transparent pass, particles (soft particles, depth-faded).
5. TAA in HDR (before tone mapping).
6. Motion blur — camera motion, plus per-object for rotors, wheels and tracks
   (see the helicopter rotor rule in 2.6).
7. Bloom in HDR, driven by physical brightness, not a threshold hack.
8. Exposure, then tone map + colour-grade LUT in the output stage.
9. Contrast-adaptive sharpening (AMD FidelityFX CAS is MIT-licensed) to win back
   texture detail that TAA softens. Strength ~0.3.
10. Film grain very low or off. Chromatic aberration off by default.
11. HUD, in its own pass, after everything. **No bloom on the HUD.**

- Colour-grade with a LUT, not with per-material colour tweaks.

### 2.6 Special cases

- **Rotor blades:** above ~50 % rotor RPM swap the blade meshes for a
  semi-transparent blurred disc with a blade-shadow texture, blended by RPM.
  TAA and camera motion blur cannot draw a 20 Hz rotor correctly.
- **Muzzle flash:** a point light for 1–2 frames, no shadow, plus an emissive
  flipbook. Values on the lighting sheet (candela).
- **Render-to-texture views** (scopes, gunner sights, MFDs, sensor turrets):
  lower resolution than the main view, their own LOD bias, shadows from the
  main cascades reused. Each one has a line in the per-game budget.

## 3. Materials and textures

### 3.1 Materials

- `MeshStandardMaterial` by default. `MeshPhysicalMaterial` only where you need
  clearcoat (wet asphalt, car paint), transmission (canopy and window glass,
  water), sheen (uniform fabric) or anisotropy (brushed aluminium).
- Values come from `pbr-material-reference.png`. The generator checks them:
  - **Non-metals:** base colour linear luminance 0.021–0.92
    (about `#282828` to `#f4f4f4`). Rubber and soot sit at the floor,
    fresh snow at the ceiling.
  - **Metals:** base colour *is* the specular colour F0, and is bright —
    never darker than about `#b4b4b4`. Copper is pale pink-orange, not brown.
  - `metalness 1` only for bare metal. Paint, parkerised/phosphate finish,
    rust, rubber and plastic are `metalness 0`. Bare metal on a painted part
    lives in an edge-wear mask.
- Wet surfaces: darker base colour, lower roughness and a clearcoat layer —
  the same texture set driven by a wetness mask, not a second material.
- Foliage: alpha-clip with `alphaToCoverage = true`, and a thin-translucency
  term so light shines through leaves from behind. `MeshStandardMaterial` has
  no such term — add it through TSL / `onBeforeCompile`. This is the biggest
  single win in forest maps at golden hour.
- Characters: skin needs subsurface scattering (at least a pre-integrated
  or screen-space approximation), eyes need a separate cornea/iris material,
  cloth uses sheen. Scanned or licensed characters only; no gore.

### 3.2 Texture set

- Per material: base colour, normal, ORM, and where needed height, emissive and
  a **thermal mask**.
- **ORM channel order (glTF and Three.js): R = AO, G = roughness,
  B = metalness.** Anything else silently swaps roughness and metalness.
- **Thermal mask:** one greyscale channel per asset marking engines, exhausts,
  barrels, tracks, bodies. Thermal and night-vision views read it with the
  heat state from game logic. Without it, thermal is only a colour filter.
- Base colour and emissive maps use `SRGBColorSpace`. **Normal, ORM, height and
  thermal maps use `NoColorSpace`.** Getting this wrong is the single most
  common cause of a scene that "looks like a game".
- Normal maps in **OpenGL convention (green = +Y)**, as glTF requires. A
  DirectX-style export (Substance default for some presets) inverts the
  lighting of every bump.
- No pure black and no pure white in a base colour map. Stay inside the range
  on the PBR sheet.

### 3.3 Texel density

| Asset | Target |
|---|---|
| First-person weapon and hands | 20–40 px/cm (the rifle fills a third of the screen) |
| Vehicles, props, building walls near the player | ~10 px/cm |
| Terrain base layers | ~5 px/cm, plus a detail layer |
| Jet-sim terrain from altitude | 1–5 m per pixel colour, plus procedural detail |

- Nothing looks fake faster than a crisp rifle on blurry mud. Check the ground
  at prone eye height (0.45 m), not at standing height.

### 3.4 Killing repetition and flatness

- **Detail maps:** a tiling detail normal (and optional detail albedo) faded in
  within ~10 m. Cheap and the main source of close-up sharpness.
- **Stochastic / hex tiling** plus a low-frequency macro-variation texture on
  every large tiled surface (terrain, walls, roads). No visible grid of repeats.
- **Triplanar mapping** on cliffs, rocks and steep terrain. No stretched UVs.
- **Height-blended splatting** on terrain: sand fills the cracks between rocks,
  mud collects in ruts, instead of a soft linear fade.
- **Parallax occlusion mapping** for brick, cobbles, rubble and bark on High.
- **Decals** are projected, PBR (base colour, normal and roughness), and
  per material: a bullet hit on concrete chips, on metal it dents and exposes
  bright metal, on wood it splinters.
- **Wear lives in masks:** edge wear, dirt in cavities, dust on top faces, mud
  on lower hull, soot after a hit. A clean vehicle is an unfinished vehicle.
  Mud and dust build up at runtime with distance driven and terrain type.

### 3.5 Compression and filtering

- KTX2 is the target for every shipped texture file (the reference games still
  ship JPEG and PNG, decoded to RGBA8: four times the memory and upload of a
  block format — a new game should not start that debt). **Generate mipmaps at encode time** — the GPU cannot generate mips
  for a compressed texture, and a KTX2 without mips shimmers.
- **UASTC** for normal maps and hero textures, encoded as linear data. Never
  ETC1S for normals — it makes blocks and banding. ETC1S only for far, low-detail
  colour maps. Check exact flags against the installed `toktx` / `basisu`
  version (e.g. `toktx --t2 --encode uastc --genmipmap`).
- Alpha-tested foliage mips must **preserve alpha coverage** (rescale alpha per
  mip level in the texture pipeline). Otherwise trees go thin and see-through
  at distance.
- Anisotropic filtering at `renderer.capabilities.getMaxAnisotropy()` (16 on the
  target machine) on everything seen at a grazing angle: ground, roads, walls,
  runways. Mipmaps always on.

## 4. Models and level of detail

- Real silhouettes, real proportions — `scale-reference.png` is the check.
- glTF/GLB only, Draco or Meshopt compressed, KTX2 textures.
- Three LODs per vehicle plus an impostor beyond 800 m. Switch on screen-space
  size, not raw distance. The scope and gunner-sight views use their
  magnification when choosing LOD — a tank at 1500 m through a 10× sight is a
  close-up.
- Instance everything repeated: trees, rubble, fences, rivets. Use
  `InstancedMesh` and a GPU cull.
- Large terrain (≥ 2 km) streams in chunks with a clipmap/CDLOD scheme and
  skirts, no cracks. Heightmaps come from an erosion-based tool, not raw noise:
  masking, hull-down play and sniper sightlines all depend on believable ridges.
- Terrain deforms where it matters: tank ruts and wheel tracks in mud, sand and
  snow, as a displacement + decal layer that fades over minutes.
- Tank tracks are animated links on a spline over the road wheels, not a
  scrolling UV on a static belt.
- Y is up. Z is forward. Models face `-Z` in their own space. Fix orientation in
  the asset, never with a wrapper rotation in code.

## 5. Physics

- One physics library for the whole project (Rapier or Ammo). Never two.
- Fixed timestep at 60 Hz, decoupled from render. Accumulate and interpolate
  transforms for the visual frame. Never feed a variable `deltaTime` to physics.
- Collision shapes are simple: boxes, capsules, convex hulls. Never a render
  mesh as a collider except for static terrain.
- Vehicle physics uses real mass and a real centre of mass, low and forward.
  A tank that pivots like a turret in a shooter is broken even if it "feels ok".
- Projectiles are ray-cast per step with drag, not kinematic tweens. Use the
  same G7 point-mass model as `ballistics-reference.png` and match its drop,
  holdover and time of flight. Lead moving targets.
- Explosions apply impulse with distance falloff and line-of-sight occlusion.

## 6. Cameras

- Every vehicle has **interior** and **exterior** cameras on one toggle key.
- Interior: real eye position, real FOV (about 60–75° vertical), the cockpit or
  crew station modelled and lit as geometry, not painted on. Interiors get
  their own baked lighting or probe and receive the sun's shadow cascades.
- First-person weapon and hands: rendered with their own FOV so they do not
  clip into walls, but they **receive world shadows and reflection probes** —
  a weapon that is never in shadow floats.
- Exterior: spring-arm follow, damped, with collision so it never clips terrain.
- Camera shake is driven by physics events (recoil, hit, rotor wash), scaled by
  a single global intensity the player can turn down.
- Never cut the camera without a reason the player can see.
- **Aiming never turns the camera.** A turret, a gun or a sensor is aimed with a
  cursor the mouse or the right thumb moves over the view; the sight line is
  the ray through that cursor. The camera stays with the vehicle, so fixed
  weapons' cues stay on its axis.
- Cockpit displays: constant output brightness (not exposed with the scene),
  mipmapped, and never coplanar with their bezel (3–4 mm proud), or they
  flicker. From every seat, nothing of the cockpit blocks the centre of view.

## 7. HUD and UI

- Follow `hud-layout-reference.png`. Title-safe 90 %. The **centre zone** is a
  circle with a diameter of 30 % of screen width: symbols only (reticle,
  flight-path marker, pitch ladder, velocity-vector ball, lead mark), **no text**.
- On 21:9 and 32:9 screens, anchor the HUD to the centred 16:9 area.
- HUD is DOM or a separate orthographic scene — not sprites in the world.
- Render crisply at the device pixel ratio. A blurry HUD ruins a sharp frame.
- Aircraft HUDs are green stroke on transparent, drawn as vector lines, and they
  **float in the world**, not on the glass: the flight-path marker tracks the
  actual velocity vector.
- Every HUD element has a real source. If nothing feeds it, delete it.
- Damage, hit and lock feedback must be readable in under 200 ms: a shape
  change, not just a colour change. Add a distinct sound for each.
- Show what the flight computer is doing: the power lever's mode (CLIMB / HOLD /
  DESC) by the collective bar, SAS state, laser range.
- A ballistic cue (rockets, bombs) is computed with the **same integrator and
  step** as the real projectile, or cue and hits disagree at grazing angles.
- Symbols behind the camera are culled in view space (`z > −0.5`), not from the
  NDC z of `project()` (wrong with a reversed depth buffer).

## 8. Audio

- Positional audio with distance falloff and a real speed of sound (343 m/s) —
  a tank round should arrive before its bang at 1500 m.
- Layer every weapon sound: mechanical action, blast, tail, and a distant
  version chosen by range.
- Engine sound blends by RPM and load, never by a single speed value.
- Duck the mix on an explosion, do not just make everything louder.
- A delayed one-shot (a far blast heard after the light) starts **silent**: set
  `gain.value = 0` before scheduling the envelope, or `start(at)` the source at
  the event — an `AudioParam` sits at its default of 1 until its first event.

## 9. Performance

- **Locked 60 fps is the requirement.** 120 is the stretch goal. See
  `performance-budget-reference.png` for the per-pass budget.
- Target **14 ms** GPU, not 16.6. The 2.6 ms gap absorbs GC, streaming and
  driver spikes. GPU and CPU are budgeted separately — they run in parallel.
- Each game spends its 2.0 ms **game-specific slot** on one thing: FPS scope
  view, jet volumetric clouds, tank gunner sight + thermal, heli sensor + MFDs
  + downwash. Anything more comes out of another line.
- Ceilings, High / Low: 2500 / 1500 draw calls, 12 / 4 M triangles,
  120 / 80 unique materials, 4 / 2 shadow casters, 8 / 2.5 GB textures,
  250 / 250 active rigid bodies. Over any of them, the fix is content, not a
  renderer setting. Physics is identical on both presets.
- No allocation in the frame loop. Pre-allocate vectors, quaternions and arrays;
  reuse them. Watch for hidden allocation in `.clone()` and array methods.
- Pool everything spawned: bullets, shells, decals, particles, sound sources.
- Profile with the Chrome performance panel and `renderer.info` before and after
  any optimisation. Put both numbers in the reply.
- Load assets in a real streaming pass with a progress bar. No frame-time spike
  on first shot because a texture decoded mid-fight. **Build every pipeline
  behind the loading screen by rendering it**: one object per shader build
  forced visible for one real frame of the full post chain, and one frame of
  every other camera (scope, sight, mirror, sensor). Not `renderer.compileAsync`:
  in r186 it hangs until its timeout and is slower than nothing.
- **Loading is budgeted and measured like the frame** (`tools/measure/load.mjs`,
  `profile.mjs`; playbook §8.4). Each stage logs `[load] <stage>: <seconds>`; the
  number that counts is when the GPU has finished what loading queued, not when
  the game says ready. Rules that came out of it: canvases drawn once are made
  in memory (`willReadFrequently: true`); instanced meshes filled once are
  marked so they share shaders (`render/instancing.ts`); a second camera's
  render target is a twin of the scene pass's; noise in load-time loops uses the
  gradient table (`core/noise.ts`); nothing two systems need is computed twice.
  State the pipeline count before and after any change that adds a material, an
  instanced mesh or a camera.
- **Phones render at 2× device pixels** (never capped at 1×) with TRAA and a
  light sharpen on every preset; the frame governor lowers the render scale
  (down to ~0.42) before anything else. A "Fast" preset at 1× is the opt-out.
- Map-scale content is generated, not listed: forests as GPU-instanced impostor
  rings driven by the land-cover texture, terrain as CDLOD from baked grids.
  Heavy bakes run on the GPU (quad passes into render targets) or on worker
  threads in bands, never as one long main-thread loop.
- Gameplay objects (targets, pickups, landing sites) are placed by querying the
  same cover function the renderer uses: never inside a generated wood where
  nobody can see them.

## 10. Code structure

- Strict TypeScript. `any` banned. Vectors and quaternions are typed, not
  loose `number[]`.
- Entity-component style: data in components, behaviour in systems, one fixed
  update order written down in one file.
- **Game logic never touches `THREE` directly.** A render system reads game
  state and writes transforms. This is what lets you test logic headlessly.
- All tunable values live in a typed config object in one place, with units and
  a comment on each. No magic numbers in a system file.
- Deterministic where possible: seeded RNG, fixed step, no reliance on
  `Date.now()` inside logic.
- Input is mapped through a rebindable layer. Never read `event.code` in a
  gameplay system.

## 10b. Controls (vehicles, aircraft)

Physics stays real; the controls make it flyable. Every rule below came from a
player's complaint.

- **Keys never flip** with an "invert" setting: W is nose down / forward, S nose
  up. Inversion is for a thumb stick only.
- A/D **turn** (mostly yaw in a hover, a coordinated bank with speed); Q/E are
  the pedals (pure yaw).
- Once the game has weapons, **the mouse aims and does not fly**. On touch, the
  right thumb aims and never feeds the flight controls.
- A **flight computer is on by default** (and can be switched off for the bare
  airframe): attitude hold on the stick, heading hold on the pedals with the
  tail rotor fed forward from rotor torque, and **height hold on the power lever
  with a neutral detent** — below it descend, at it hold, above it climb. It
  respects the torque limit, protects against the vortex ring at low speed, and
  flares to a soft touchdown. The touch lever springs back to the detent.
- Fixed weapons fire **along the vehicle's axis**, not carried off by its drift.
- Tilt steering can be switched on in play (and recalibrated), not only in the
  menu.
- Every control behaviour has a unit test in the simulation (hover, climb /
  hold / descend, landing, heading through lever snatches, key mapping). Do not
  judge it from a headless browser: a software renderer runs the simulation at
  a fraction of real time.

## 11. Content and conduct

These are war-themed action games, built as entertainment. Keep them there.

- Vehicles, weapons and effects are modelled for **looks and feel**. Never write
  real engineering, weapon construction, explosive, or targeting-system detail
  into this repo, even in a comment. Public spec numbers for size, speed and
  silhouette are fine — that is what the reference sheets are for.
- Use invented unit names, invented markings and generic factions. Do not
  recreate a specific real battle, a real named person, or a real atrocity.
- Damage feedback is mechanical: armour penetration, module hits, smoke, fire,
  wreck. No gore, no mutilation, no lingering on bodies.
- No real trademarks, logos, liveries or insignia in shipped assets.

## 12. Definition of done for a game feature

1. It runs at 60 fps on the target machine with the budget sheet respected.
2. It looks right against the PBR, scale and lighting references — checked with
   a screenshot next to the sheet, at noon, golden hour and overcast.
3. It works in both interior and exterior camera.
4. It has sound.
5. It has failure feedback the player can read in a second.
6. The tunables are in config, with units.
7. You ran it and watched it, and you say so in the reply.
